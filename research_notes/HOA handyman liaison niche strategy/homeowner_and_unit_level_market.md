# Direct-to-Homeowner (B2C) Handyman Market and the Unit-Level Resident Market (US, Utah emphasis, as of Oct 2026)

Research method note: ~45 web searches were run; direct page fetches (JCHS, Angi IR, HomeAdvisor, Thumbtack, Levelset, Utah Business) were blocked by the session's egress proxy, so most figures below come from search-result extracts of those pages and from secondary reporting of them. Confidence levels reflect that. "National" is labeled where data is not Utah-specific. Figures older than 2023 are labeled.

## Key Question 1: US home services / handyman market size and growth; repair/maintenance spend per household

### Takeaway
The broadly defined US "handyman services" industry is roughly $355B (IBISWorld, 2025) across ~550K mostly tiny businesses, growing ~1% in 2025 after a 3.7% five-year CAGR; the typical homeowner spent about $2,000 on maintenance in 2025 (Angi), while total improvement+repair spending is heavily skewed to higher-income owners (JCHS: top-quintile owners spend ~3-4x bottom-quintile owners).

### Cited Findings
- IBISWorld estimates US Handyman Services industry revenue at $355.3B in 2025, up 0.8% in 2025 (2024: $352.4B), with a 3.7% CAGR for 2020-2025 and 549,688 businesses in 2025 (+1.1%). An older IBISWorld snapshot showed $273.6B for 2024 and a third-party guide cited $368.2B for 2025, so the series is unstable; the $355.3B figure is from the current IBISWorld market-size page. Note IBISWorld's "handyman" definition is broad (general repair/maintenance) and far exceeds what a small-job handyman niche actually addresses. Confidence: medium (paywalled; headline only). — [IBISWorld market size](https://img3.ibisworld.com/united-states/market-size/handyman-services/4069); [IBISWorld number of businesses](https://www.ibisworld.com/industry-statistics/number-of-businesses/handyman-services-united-states/)
- Angi 2025 State of Home Spending (published Jan 20, 2026): average maintenance spend $2,041 per household in 2025, up from $1,750 in 2024 (which was itself down from $2,458 in 2023); homeowners averaged $12,472 total on home projects in 2025 (+3.5% YoY) and completed ~10 projects (vs 9 in 2024). Millennials had the highest maintenance spend ($2,601) and total spend ($14,199); Gen Z spent the least overall ($10,283) but ranked second in maintenance ($2,261). National. Confidence: medium-high (figures from press release extracts; full PDF not retrievable). — [Angi IR PDF](https://ir.angi.com/node/12961/pdf); [Finviz summary](https://finviz.com/news/281088/millennials-are-driving-the-future-of-home-spending-according-to-angis-state-of-home-spending-report); [HBS Dealer](https://hbsdealer.com/state-home-spending-report)
- Angi 2024 State of Home Spending (released Jan 2025): total home project spending fell 12% in 2024; 93% of homeowners planned projects in 2025; 54% struggle to find qualified pros (delays, higher costs, limited availability). National. Confidence: medium-high. — [KBB Online](https://kbbonline.com/?p=159593); [Apartment Therapy](https://www.apartmenttherapy.com/angi-state-of-home-spending-report-2024-37454815)
- Angi 2025 Pulse Report (May 2025): 71% of homeowners had delayed a planned project that year; 48% said stress from home repairs increased since January; 71% say they now focus on preventative maintenance. National. Confidence: medium. — [KBB Online](https://kbbonline.com/?p=161984)
- Harvard JCHS (2023 AHS data, Improving America's Housing 2025): owners in the bottom income fifth (<$37,500) spent an average of $2,300 on improvements vs $9,100 for the top fifth (>$172,000); highest-income owners spent nearly 3x as much on DIY and more than 4x as much on professional installations as the lowest-income owners. National. Confidence: high. — [JCHS blog: interactive tool](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/interactive-tool-highlights-disparities-remodeling-spending)
- JCHS (2023 AHS): bottom-quintile owners spent an average of $3,100 on improvements and repairs (a broader measure than the $2,300 improvements-only figure above); 28% of lowest-quintile owners spent nothing on improvements or maintenance vs 14% of higher-income owners; 14% of lower-income owners spent $10,000+ vs 38% of higher-income owners; for pre-1960 homes, top-quintile owners spent $12,700 vs $3,400 for bottom-quintile. Owners overall spent 7.5% of income on improvements/repairs. National. Confidence: high. — [JCHS blog: home repairs out of reach](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/home-repairs-are-out-reach-many-lower-income-homeowners)
- JCHS 2025 report: replacement projects (roofing, windows, HVAC) accounted for 49% of improvement spending in 2023. National. Confidence: high. — [JCHS report PDF](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/reports/files/Harvard_JCHS_Improving_Americas_Housing_2025.pdf)
- Houzz & Home 2026 (covering 2025): 54% of US homeowners undertook renovations; median spend held at $20,000; 50% plan renovations (down from 52%). National. Confidence: medium-high. — [Hardwood Floors Mag](https://hardwoodfloorsmag.com/2026/05/01/home-renovation-activity-held-steady-in-2025/)
- Instant Roofer analysis of AHS data (reported May 2025): routine maintenance (gutter cleaning, basic HVAC service, etc.) costs US households ~$1,356/yr, 1.7% of typical household income. National; not a JCHS source. Confidence: low-medium. — [The Mortgage Point](https://themortgagepoint.com/2025/05/02/where-are-home-improvements-least-affordable/)
- Jobber Home Service Economic Report (Feb 2025, "2024 Review and 2025 Outlook"): home service revenue growth was "fueled through larger invoice sizes and pricing adjustments" while consumer demand was "volatile but resilient"; a Feb 2026 edition ("2025 Review and 2026 Outlook") draws on 350,000+ users but its figures were not retrievable. National. Confidence: medium. — [Jobber Feb 2025 PDF](https://www.getjobber.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/Jobber-HomeServiceEconomicReport-Feb2025.pdf); [Jobber Feb 2026](https://www.getjobber.com/home-service-reports/february-2026)

### Inferences
- The addressable small-job handyman niche (TV mounting, furniture assembly, minor repairs) is a small slice of IBISWorld's $355B; a better planning anchor is Angi's ~$2,000/household/yr maintenance spend multiplied by target households, of which only a fraction is outsourced to a handyman (vs HVAC, plumbing, lawn).
- Utah's homeowner base is unusually young (see Q8); Angi's finding that Millennials and Gen Z spend the most on maintenance per household is favorable for a Utah handyman niche if those owners outsource rather than DIY.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve Angi's 2025 report PDF or JCHS Tables A-8 to A-10, so repair/maintenance spend broken out by home value band (e.g., $400K vs $1M homes) was not obtained.
- No handyman-specific (as opposed to all home services) revenue figure for Utah or the Salt Lake-Provo metro was found.
- HomeAdvisor "True Cost Report" figures for 2024-2026 were not surfaced by searches.

## Key Question 2: Propensity to hire vs DIY by income, age and home value; do million-dollar-home owners hire far more than $400K-home owners?

### Takeaway
DIY's share of improvement spending is at a multi-decade low (discretionary DIY share fell from 31.5% in 1995 to 19.1% in 2023; pro spending +14% and DIY -13% in 2021-23), ~90% of renovating homeowners hire a pro, and high-income owners spend 4x+ more on professional installs than low-income owners; direct evidence comparing $1M-home owners to $400K-home owners on handyman hiring specifically was not found.

### Cited Findings
- JCHS: the DIY share of all improvement spending fell to a period low in 2023; from 2021 to 2023, real spending on professionally installed projects rose 14% while DIY spending fell 13%; discretionary projects' DIY share fell from 31.5% (1995) to 19.1% (2023). The decline has varied by homeowner age and was much steeper for discretionary than replacement work. DIY spending counts only materials, pro spending includes labor/overhead, so dollar shares understate DIY activity. National. Confidence: high. — [JCHS blog: shifted away from DIY](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/home-improvement-spending-has-shifted-away-diy); [JCHS IAH 2025 DIY page](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/iah-2025-diy-professional-spending)
- JCHS: top-income-quintile owners spent nearly 3x as much on DIY and more than 4x as much on professional installation as bottom-quintile owners (2023). National. Confidence: high. — [JCHS blog](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/blog/interactive-tool-highlights-disparities-remodeling-spending)
- Houzz & Home 2026 (2025 renovations): 91% of renovating homeowners hire professionals; the 2025 study (2024 renovations) found 90%, with 49% hiring specialty service providers. National. Confidence: medium-high. — [Hardwood Floors Mag](https://hardwoodfloorsmag.com/2026/05/01/home-renovation-activity-held-steady-in-2025/); [Wood Floor Business](https://www.woodfloorbusiness.com/news/article/15743778/houzz-homeowners-spent-20000-on-renovations-in-2024)
- Angi 2026 "Home Oops" report (1,000 US homeowners): 78% experienced a home "oops"; 52% of DIY mistakes required a professional to finish. National. Confidence: medium. — [Finviz / Angi release](https://finviz.com/news/402475/diy-detours-and-uninvited-raccoons-angi-reveals-americas-home-oops-report)
- Home Depot FY2025: Pro customers represent roughly half of revenue (third-party estimates; no official split surfaced); Q1 2025 Pro comps outpaced DIY, Q2 both roughly in line, Q3 "DIY trends remain challenged as customers pull back on discretionary spending." National. Confidence: medium. — [Nasdaq: Home Depot Pro momentum](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/home-depots-pro-momentum-enough-offset-diy-slowdowns); [Distribution Strategy Q1](https://distributionstrategy.com/2025/05/home-depot-q1-sales-rise-9-4-as-distributors-and-contractors-drive-growth/)
- Lowe's: Pro is ~25% of sales vs more than 50% at Home Depot (2025 analyst commentary). National. Confidence: medium. — [Nasdaq: Pro vs DIY Lowe's](https://www.nasdaq.com/articles/pro-vs-diy-which-customer-segment-will-power-lowes-2025)
- Angi 2025 SOHS generational data: Millennials spent most on maintenance ($2,601) and emergencies ($1,519); Gen Z second in both ($2,261 / $1,400). National. Confidence: medium-high. — [Finviz](https://finviz.com/news/281088/millennials-are-driving-the-future-of-home-spending-according-to-angis-state-of-home-spending-report)
- Angi (2024-25): 54% of homeowners struggle to find qualified pros. National. Confidence: medium-high. — [KBB Online](https://kbbonline.com/?p=159593)

### Inferences
- Because the pro-spending gap between top- and bottom-income owners (4x+) is larger than the DIY gap (~3x), higher-income owners outsource a larger share of their work, not just more total work. This supports targeting higher-value neighborhoods (Holladay, Draper, Park City) for a hire-rather-than-DIY customer base.
- The JCHS income gradient is for improvements/repairs broadly. Applying it to small handyman jobs is an extrapolation; small jobs are the most DIY-able category, so the hire-vs-DIY gap for TV mounting may be larger than for roofing (where almost everyone hires) but no data confirms this.
- No source quantifies "million-dollar-home owners hire handymen X times more than $400K-home owners." Income (not home value) is the variable JCHS reports; in Utah, home value and income are correlated but the correlation is loosened by the state's rapid appreciation (many $600K+ homes are owned by moderate-income young families).

### Gaps
- JCHS DIY share by homeowner age (e.g., under-35 vs 55+) was referenced but the exact percentages could not be retrieved (pages blocked).
- No Thumbtack or Angi survey found that splits hire-vs-DIY for small jobs by income or home value.
- No Utah-specific hire-vs-DIY data found.

## Key Question 3: Customer acquisition cost for homeowner leads; close rates; share of revenue from repeat/referral

### Takeaway
Handyman leads are among the cheapest in home services (roughly $10-30 per shared lead on Angi/Thumbtack/LSA, vs ~$53 blended home-services LSA average), but shared-lead close rates of ~6-20% push effective cost per won job to roughly $100-300, which a $150-250 handyman ticket cannot recover on the first visit; mature handyman businesses report 50-65%+ of revenue from repeat clients.

### Cited Findings
- Angi Leads (formerly HomeAdvisor): ~$300/yr membership plus $15-$85 per lead depending on trade/location; small handyman jobs at the ~$15 floor; leads sold to 3-8 contractors simultaneously; 12-month auto-renewing contracts. In Jan 2025 Angi moved to a "homeowner choice" model where homeowners select which pros contact them; network revenue fell sharply afterward with management projecting a return to growth in 2026. The FTC previously obtained a $7.2M settlement with HomeAdvisor over deceptive marketing of leads. National; third-party sources, not Angi's price list. Confidence: medium-low. — [Phonely: Is Angi Leads worth it 2025](https://www.phonely.ai/blog/is-angi-leads-worth-it--what-contractors-really-need-to-know); [Ecohome: Angi Leads analysis](https://ecohome.co/articles/angi-leads); [Hook Agency](https://hookagency.com/blog/angi-leads-reviews/)
- Other Angi estimates: $15-$100 per lead; garage-door marketer says leads resold to 3-5 companies at $25-75+; a competitor claims $1,400+ effective cost per booked job (treat with caution). Trustpilot contractor rating ~1.6/5 from 37,200+ reviews with complaints about $100+ shared leads and leads from people with no intention to hire. Confidence: low-medium. — [Jobber: HomeAdvisor vs Angi](https://getjobber.com/academy/homeadvisor-vs-angi/); [Marketing360](https://www.marketing360.com/trends/is-angi-homeadvisor-still-worth-it-for-garage-door-leads); [Trustpilot Angi](https://ch.trustpilot.com/review/angi.com)
- Thumbtack: does not publish lead prices; handyman leads reported at under $10 for simple jobs, $8-25 for a day of handyman work, and $10-75 across handyman categories; prices change weekly (posted Fridays for the following week) and rise with job size, market, and competing pros; pros set a max lead price and weekly budget; one pro reported a $154 lead on a $400-700 job when they were the only pro selected, ~$50 when three were selected. Confidence: low-medium. — [Enrich Labs LSA guide 2026](https://www.enrichlabs.ai/blog/google-local-service-ads-complete-guide-2026); [PipelineOn LSA for home service](https://pipelineon.com/blog/google-local-services-ads-home-service/); [Adapt Digital: LSA for contractors](https://adaptdigitalsolutions.com/articles/google-local-service-ads-for-contractors/)

National. Confidence: low. — [BizQuest listing 1](https://www.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/high-demand-home-repair-business-immediate-income/BW2467390/); [BizQuest listing 2](https://www.bizquest.com/business-for-sale/profitable-and-established-handyman-business-with-high-net-income/BW2467382/); [Handyman Startup blog](https://www.handymanstartup.com/blog/page/9/); [WiFi Talents](https://wifitalents.com/handyman-industry-statistics/)

### Inferences
- Worked CAC: a $20 shared handyman lead closing at 10-15% implies ~$130-200 per won customer; at $15/lead and 20% close, ~$75. Against a $150-250 ticket with a 20% coordination fee ($30-50 gross), paid-lead acquisition is unrecoverable on the first job and only works with 3+ repeat jobs per customer. An HOA/property-manager referral channel that delivers customers at near-zero marginal CAC is therefore structurally far more attractive than marketplaces for low-ticket work.
- Referral-sourced leads close at 3-5x the rate of marketplace leads (40-60% vs 8-15% in the plumbing example), which further compounds the advantage of an HOA-endorsed channel.

### Gaps
- No Utah/Salt Lake-specific lead prices for Angi, Thumbtack or LSA were found; all pricing is national and third-party.
- No independent survey quantifying the repeat/referral revenue share for handyman businesses; only seller claims and blogger assertions.
- Angi's post-2025 "homeowner choice" per-lead pricing is not published; current prices must be checked in the Angi Pro app.

## Key Question 4: Nonpayment, chargeback and dispute rates for residential handyman work; homeowner vs commercial payment behavior; mitigation

### Takeaway
Residential customers pay faster and more reliably than commercial/public clients (Levelset: residential contractors are paid within 30 days 48% of the time vs 21% for public work, and 60+ day delays occur only 6% vs 18%), chargeback rates in home services are reportedly elevated toward the 1% monitoring threshold versus a ~0.26% all-industry average, but no source quantifies the share of handyman jobs that end in outright nonpayment.

### Cited Findings
- Levelset 2022 Construction Cash Flow & Payment Report (519 US construction companies): residential contractors report being paid within 30 days 48% of the time vs 21% for public-project contractors; 60-day-or-longer delays happen 6% of the time for residential vs 18% for public; only 12% of all companies say they are always paid on time; residential contractors attribute prompt payment to interpersonal communication and quality work, while commercial/public contractors cite process factors. 2022 data (labeled older). National. Confidence: high for the survey, medium for applicability to handymen. — [Equipment World](https://www.equipmentworld.com/business/article/15292311/report-construction-industry-rife-with-payment-issues); [Pro Builder](https://www.probuilder.com/construction/labor-trade-relations/article/55219423/residential-contractors-more-likely-to-be-paid-on-time-than-commercial-contractors); [Levelset 2022 report](https://www.levelset.com/tools/2022-construction-cash-flow-payment-report/)
- Levelset 2021 (764 professionals): only 9% always paid on time; 2019: 50% not paid on time, 42% always front labor/material costs before being paid. Older. — [Levelset 2021 report](https://www.levelset.com/tools/2021-national-construction-payments-report/); [Construction Dive](https://www.constructiondive.com/news/report-levelset-survey-general-contractor-subs-construction-payment/623001/)
- US small businesses generally (Gateway Commercial Finance 2025, 503 owners, not home-service-specific): 64% have invoices more than 90 days overdue. Confidence: low relevance. — [Small Biz Trends](https://smallbiztrends.com/late-payments-small-business-cash-flow-crisis/)
- UK tradespeople (Ford Pro research, 2025, not US): ~£3.5B owed in overdue payments; almost two-thirds experience late payments regularly, average delay 35 days; 14% of UK homeowners admit to having forgotten to pay a tradesperson altogether. Confidence: medium (UK only). — [Professional Builder UK](https://probuildermag.co.uk/?p=42229)
- Chargebacks: Sift Q3 2025 all-industry average 0.26% of transactions (up from 0.21%); chargeback.io cites 0.60% average with verticals ranging 0.12-1.02%; SwipeSimple (payments vendor) claims home services businesses "often face higher chargeback rates than traditional retail, with many contractors approaching the critical 1% monitoring threshold," $191 average cost per dispute, 75% "friendly fraud"; Chargeflow research puts the Home & Garden dispute win rate at 20.01% vs ~30% merchant average. National; vendor sources. Confidence: low-medium. — [SwipeSimple home services chargebacks](https://home.swipesimple.com/connect/blog/chargeback-prevention-for-home-services-hvac-plumbing-electrical-landscaping); [Chargeback.io benchmarks](https://www.chargeback.io/zh/blog/average-e-commerce-chargeback-rate); [Chargeback.io win rates](https://www.chargeback.io/it/blog/how-often-do-merchants-win-chargeback-disputes)
- Mitigation guidance: Western Australia consumer regulator advises deposits of no more than ~10%, balance on satisfactory completion, staged payments for larger jobs (regulator guidance, not US). A BC tribunal held a contractor who substantially completes is entitled to payment with set-off for proven deficiencies. Forum practitioners report pushing for 50% deposits for labor-only work and that large corporates, not small customers, are the slowest payers. Confidence: low (anecdotal/non-US). — [WA Consumer Protection](https://consumerprotection.wa.gov.au/announcements/10000-fine-not-so-handy-handyman-walter-vermeulen-ratio-design); [CRT BC decision](https://decisions.civilresolutionbc.ca/crt/crtd/en/524961/1/document.do); [4x4 Community forum](https://www.4x4community.co.za/forum/showthread.php?p=2111245)
- Second Nature resident-benefits case study: a property manager reported a 78% lower late-payment rate than the national average after bundling a resident benefits package (rent payments, not handyman invoices). Vendor claim. — [Second Nature RPM First Class](https://www.secondnature.com/customer-stories/rpm-firstclass)

### Inferences
- The best available evidence says homeowners (B2C) are better payers than B2B/commercial accounts on speed; this contradicts a common assumption that B2C is riskier. For a coordination model, the realistic risk is not slow invoices but card disputes ("friendly fraud") on small, instantly paid tickets, where win rates are poor (~20%).
- For $100-300 jobs, pay-on-completion by card with a card-on-file authorization at booking (and photo documentation of completed work for dispute evidence) is the mitigation most consistent with both the regulator guidance (small deposit, pay when done) and the chargeback data.

### Gaps
- No US survey (Jobber, Housecall Pro, Thumbtack, NAHB) quantifying the percentage of residential handyman jobs that end in nonpayment or dispute was found; the search budget ran out before Jobber's payment-timing data could be checked.
- Reddit r/Handyman threads on nonpayment did not surface in results; practitioner nonpayment frequency remains anecdotal.
- No home-services-specific chargeback rate from a card network or processor dataset.

## Key Question 5: Handyman ticket size and hourly rates in Utah / Salt Lake City; TV mounting price; margin on a 20% coordination fee for a $200 job

### Takeaway
Salt Lake area TV mounting averages ~$229 (HomeAdvisor range $148-288) with TaskRabbit taskers starting at $45/hr on a 2-hour minimum; handyman hourly rates run ~$50-100 (typical ~$65) with $60-100 minimums; on a $200 job a 20% coordination fee yields $40 gross, ~$34 after processing the full ticket through Stripe, and roughly $20-28 after modest admin time.

- Angi describes handyman small tasks such as TV mounting and furniture assembly as "typically costing under $500" (2018 Handy acquisition coverage; older). — [IBJ](https://www.ibj.com/articles/70878-angi-homeservices-buying-handy-in-effort-to-dominate-home-renovations)

### Inferences
- Margin math on a $200 job with a 20% coordination fee (own calculation using the Stripe rate above): gross fee $40. If the coordinator collects the full $200 by card: processing $200 x 2.9% + $0.30 = $6.10, leaving $33.90 (17.0% of ticket). If the handyman collects and only the $40 fee is processed: $1.46 in fees, leaving $38.54. Admin (quote, scheduling, confirmations, follow-up) at 20-30 minutes valued at $25-30/hr costs $8-15, leaving roughly $19-26 net (full-ticket processing) or $24-30 (fee-only). A single Square Free invoice (3.3% + $0.30) would cut another ~$0.80.
- At ~$20-30 net per job, break-even on a $130-200 marketplace CAC requires 5-10 jobs per acquired customer; at near-zero referral CAC (HOA channel), the fee is profitable from the first job. TV mounting at the SLC average of $229 gives $46 gross / ~$39 after full-ticket processing.
- Volume needed: to net $5,000/month on coordination fees alone at ~$25/job requires ~200 jobs/month (~10 per working day), which is a dispatch-scale operation, not a side project; the handyman's own labor margin (not the fee) is where the economics of low-ticket jobs actually live.

### Gaps
- No Utah-specific handyman hourly-rate median (Thumbtack/Angi show national defaults on local pages); the HomeAdvisor North Salt Lake handyman cost page could not be fetched.
- No data on average handyman invoice size in Utah (Housecall Pro/Jobber invoice data not retrieved before the search budget ended).

## Key Question 7: Definitions — is a townhome "single-family"? Census/HUD/NAHB vs legal (condo vs townhome)

### Takeaway
For Census construction statistics a townhouse is "single-family (attached)" only if it meets a structural test (ground-to-roof wall, own heating and utility meters, no units above/below); condominium is a form of ownership, not a structure type, so a condo is classified by its building; legally a condo owner holds title to interior airspace with common ownership of land/exterior, while a fee-simple townhome owner holds the land under and around the unit (though townhomes can also be sold as condos).

### Cited Findings
- US Census Bureau (Survey of Construction definitions): single-family structures include fully detached, semi-detached, row houses, duplexes, quadruplexes and townhouses, provided each unit is separated by a ground-to-roof wall, has its own heating system and utility meters, and has no units above or below; otherwise the building is multifamily. "Condominiums and cooperatives are considered types of home ownership, and this survey is only concerned with the structure"; a condo structure meeting the single-family test is single-family, otherwise multifamily. Confidence: high. — [Census SOC definitions](https://www.census.gov/construction/soc/definitions.html); [Census Building Permits class FAQ](https://www.census.gov/construction/bps/faqs/class_faq.html)
- Census multifamily design types: "Townhouse – side by side housing units that do not meet the definition of single-family houses" (e.g., stacked or shared-utility townhomes) vs "Conventional Apartments – units above and below each other." Confidence: high. — [Census SOC definitions](https://www.census.gov/construction/soc/definitions.html)
- Local government practice (Loudoun County, VA): "Single-Family Attached (SFA)" covers townhomes, duplexes, triplexes and quadplexes where each unit has its own ground-floor exterior entrance; "stacked townhomes" are counted as multifamily. Confidence: medium (one jurisdiction). — [Loudoun housing unit type definitions](https://www.loudoun.gov/DocumentCenter/View/194873/Housing-Unit-Type-Definitions)
- Legal distinction: condo owners "hold title only to the interior airspace of their unit; the exterior walls, roof, and land are common property managed by the HOA"; a fee-simple townhome owner holds "the interior and exterior, the land beneath and around the property, and some rights to the air space above"; a townhouse "can be either owned outright ... known as Fee Simple, or it could be owned as a condominium"; both typically have an HOA and share at least one wall. Confidence: medium-high (consistent across real-estate sources). — [Opendoor condo vs townhome 2026](https://www.opendoor.com/articles/pros-and-cons-of-buying-condo-or-townhome); [Liz Moore](https://info.lizmoore.com/blog/bid/392525/What-is-the-Difference-Between-a-Condominium-and-a-Townhome); [Updater](https://updater.com/moving-tips/townhouse-versus-condo)

### Inferences
- For a handyman-liaison niche the operative distinction is responsibility, not statistics: in a condo the HOA owns everything outside the unit's interior airspace, so "in-unit" work is clearly the resident's; in a fee-simple townhome the owner is typically responsible for the exterior too (unless CC&Rs assign it to the HOA), so the handyman's in-unit scope can expand to exterior items.
- Both townhome owners and condo owners are "homeowners" for B2C purposes; apartment residents are renters whose in-unit work is split between landlord-responsible repairs (maintenance staff) and resident-elective tasks (TV mounting, assembly), which is the slice resident-perks programs target.

### Gaps
- HUD and NAHB definitional pages were not directly retrieved; the HUD American Housing Survey uses "single-family attached" similarly to Census but this was not confirmed from a HUD source in this session.
- Utah Condominium Ownership Act and Community Association Act specifics (Utah Code 57-8 and 57-8a) on maintenance responsibility were not researched.
